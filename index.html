Article 1

Introduction and history

Why DC–DC conversion is needed, how switching converters differ from linear regulators, and how the field evolved from mechanical switches to wide-bandgap semiconductors.

Why DC–DC conversion is needed

Almost no source delivers electrical energy at exactly the voltage its load needs. A photovoltaic module produces a few tens of volts, while the DC link of a grid-connected inverter or a DC microgrid is typically regulated at around 380–400 V. A lithium-ion cell supplies about 3.7 V, while a processor core runs at about 1 V. An electric vehicle carries a high-voltage traction battery but still needs a 12 V bus for its auxiliaries, and data centers distribute power at 48 V before converting it to the low voltages of their processors.

A DC–DC converter bridges these gaps. It raises (steps up) or lowers (steps down) a DC voltage, keeps the output regulated while the input voltage and the load change, and, when needed, provides galvanic isolation between input and output.

Linear versus switching conversion

The simplest way to lower a DC voltage is a linear regulator, in which a transistor operates as a controlled resistor and drops the excess voltage. The price is efficiency: the difference between input and output voltage is converted into heat, so the efficiency of a linear regulator cannot exceed Vout/Vin. Converting 12 V to 3.3 V this way wastes more than 70% of the input power. A linear regulator also cannot step a voltage up.

A switching converter works differently. Its semiconductor switches are either fully on or fully off, so they ideally dissipate no power, and inductors and capacitors store and release energy between the switching instants. The output voltage is controlled by the duty cycle D, the fraction of each switching period in which the main switch is on.

Two principles behind every converter. In steady state, the average voltage across each inductor over one switching period is zero (volt-second balance), and the average current through each capacitor is zero (charge balance). These two principles give the voltage conversion ratio of any converter, for example Vout = D·Vin for the buck converter and Vout = Vin/(1 − D) for the boost converter.

Practical switching converters routinely exceed 90% efficiency, and because they operate at tens of kilohertz to several megahertz, their magnetic components are far smaller than line-frequency transformers.

How switching converters evolved

  1. 1830s–1910Mechanical switching

    Induction coils used interrupted currents to generate high voltages. In 1910, Kettering’s inductive ignition system went into production for Cadillac. It is, in essence, a mechanically switched flyback boost converter in which the ignition coil acts as the transformer.

  2. 1930sVibrator power supplies

    Car radios with vacuum tubes needed plate voltages of roughly 90–250 V from a 6 V battery. An electromechanical vibrator chopped the battery voltage, and a transformer and rectifier produced the high DC voltage, the direct ancestor of the switched-mode power supply.

  3. 1950sThe transistor replaces the vibrator

    Semiconductor switches made converters silent, durable, and far more reliable. A transistor oscillation-and-rectifying converter was patented in 1959 by engineers at General Motors.

  4. 1960sThe aerospace push

    The need for small, light, and efficient power supplies drove rapid development. The Apollo Guidance Computer used early switched-mode power supplies.

  5. 1970sFrom niche to mainstream

    Switching supplies entered instruments, calculators, and early personal computers. In 1976, the first integrated circuit for switched-mode power supply control, the SG1524, was introduced, and in 1977 the Apple II used a switched-mode power supply.

  6. 1975–1976A theory for switching converters

    Slobodan Ćuk invented the Ćuk converter and, with R. D. Middlebrook at Caltech, introduced state-space averaging. By averaging the circuit over the switch states, this method gave designers linear models for analysis and control design that are still used today.

  7. 1980s–1990sFaster devices and wider conversion ratios

    Power MOSFETs allowed much higher switching frequencies than bipolar transistors, shrinking magnetics further. At the same time, researchers sought converters with large conversion ratios without transformers, a line of work that led to quadratic (cascaded) converters.

  8. 2000s–todayRenewables and wide-bandgap devices

    Photovoltaic systems, fuel cells, electric vehicles, and DC microgrids created a strong demand for high step-up, highly efficient, and reliable converters. Switched-capacitor and switched-inductor cells, voltage multipliers, coupled inductors, and hybrid quadratic structures are now active research areas, while gallium nitride (GaN) and silicon carbide (SiC) transistors push switching frequency, efficiency, and power density even higher.

Today’s design challenges

Modern DC–DC converters are judged by several competing criteria: voltage gain, efficiency, power density, voltage and current stresses on the devices, electromagnetic compatibility, cost, and, increasingly, reliability over a lifetime of tens of years. The other sections of this site address these criteria directly: Inductor Design for the magnetic components, and Loss & Reliability for efficiency and lifetime.

Practical guide

Building a laboratory boost converter

A practical guide to turning a converter design into a working laboratory prototype, from the first specifications to the final efficiency measurement.

1. Overview and preparation

Define the specifications. Input voltage range, output voltage, rated power, switching frequency, and the allowable current and voltage ripples. These numbers drive every later decision.

Design and simulate first. Calculate the inductances and capacitances, and simulate the converter (for example in PLECS, PSIM, or MATLAB/Simulink) before buying any parts. Simulation shows the expected voltage and current stresses of every component and catches design errors cheaply. The Inductor Design and Loss & Reliability tools on this site can be used at this stage.

Select the components with margin.

  • MOSFETs and diodes should have voltage ratings about 1.5 to 2 times the expected stress, and current ratings well above the RMS and peak currents.
  • Use fast- or ultrafast-recovery diodes (for example MUR1560) or Schottky diodes, since reverse recovery causes large losses at high frequency.
  • Choose capacitors for both their voltage rating and their ripple-current rating.
  • Check that the inductor core does not saturate at the peak current.

Plan the system. A laboratory converter has five blocks: the power stage, the gate drivers, the controller that generates the pulses, the auxiliary supplies for the drivers and controller, and the measurement points. Draw a block diagram and a bill of materials before starting.

2. Building and soldering the board

Choose the construction method. A prototyping board is quick for low power, but a printed circuit board (PCB) gives much lower parasitic inductance and is strongly recommended at tens of kilohertz and hundreds of watts.

Layout rules that matter most

  • Keep the high-frequency current loop, formed by the switch, the diode, and the output capacitor, as small as possible. Its parasitic inductance causes voltage spikes and ringing.
  • Use wide traces or copper pours for high-current paths.
  • Keep each gate-drive loop short, and place the driver close to its MOSFET.
  • Separate the power ground and the signal ground, and connect them at a single point.
  • Place decoupling capacitors directly at the supply pins of the drivers and the controller.
  • Keep enough spacing (creepage and clearance) around high-voltage nodes.
  • Mount the MOSFETs and diodes on heat sinks, using insulating pads where the tab is not at heat-sink potential.

Soldering. Solder in order of height: small components first, then through-hole parts, then heavy components such as inductors, capacitors, and heat sinks. Use flux, and inspect every joint. Before applying any power, check with a multimeter for short circuits between the supply rails, between the gate, drain, and source of each MOSFET, and across every capacitor.

Winding the inductors. Wind the calculated number of turns tightly and evenly around the core. Measure the inductance with an LCR meter and adjust the turns if needed. Where possible, also check the inductance under DC bias, since powder cores lose permeability at high current.

3. Control and pulse generation

Start in open loop. Generate a fixed-frequency PWM signal with a fixed duty cycle first. Closed-loop regulation can be added once the power stage is proven.

Use a hardware timer. A microcontroller timer (for example on an STM32) produces a stable PWM signal at the chosen switching frequency, with a resolution fine enough for small duty-cycle steps. If the converter has more than one switch driven by the same signal, generate the pulses from one timer so they stay synchronized.

Protect the converter in firmware.

  • Limit the maximum duty cycle. A boost converter’s gain and inductor current rise steeply at high duty cycles.
  • Use a soft start. Ramp the duty cycle gradually from a small value to the target, to avoid large inrush currents into the output capacitors.
  • Add a shutdown input so the pulses can be stopped immediately.

Check before connecting. Verify the frequency, duty cycle, and pulse shape on an oscilloscope before connecting the controller to the drivers.

4. Gate drivers

Why a driver is needed. A microcontroller output provides only 3.3 V and a few milliamperes, while a power MOSFET needs about 10–15 V at its gate and peak currents of the order of amperes to charge its gate capacitance quickly. The gate driver provides this voltage and current.

Types of drivers

  • Low-side drivers (for example the UCC27511 or TC4420) are the simplest option when the MOSFET source is connected to ground.
  • Bootstrap high-side drivers (for example the IR2110) drive a MOSFET with a floating source from a single supply.
  • Optocoupler drivers (for example the TLP250) isolate the controller from the power stage. When the MOSFET source is not at ground, each such driver needs its own isolated supply.

Gate-circuit details

  • A gate resistor of a few to a few tens of ohms sets the switching speed. A smaller value lowers switching loss; a larger one reduces ringing and EMI.
  • A resistor of about 10 kΩ between gate and source keeps the MOSFET off when the driver is unpowered.
  • A Zener diode between gate and source can clamp voltage spikes.

Check the gate waveforms with the power stage unpowered: clean, fast edges at the correct voltage, with no significant ringing.

5. Running the experiment

Safety first. The output of a high step-up converter can reach hundreds of volts, and its capacitors keep their charge after the power is removed. Use bleeder resistors across high-voltage capacitors, discharge them before touching the board, keep one hand away from the circuit when it is energized, and use a current-limited laboratory supply.

Test step by step

  1. Power-off checks: check for short circuits and verify component polarities.
  2. Control and driver test: power the controller and drivers only, and check all gate signals.
  3. Low-voltage test: apply a small input voltage (a few volts) with a light load and a tight current limit. Check that the output voltage follows the expected gain.
  4. Waveform check: measure the gate voltage, the drain-source voltage of each switch, the diode voltages, and the inductor currents, and compare them with the theoretical waveforms and simulation.
  5. Rated operation: raise the input voltage gradually to its rated value, watching the waveforms and temperatures at every step.
  6. Measurements: measure input and output voltages and currents simultaneously to calculate the efficiency, repeat at several load levels to build an efficiency curve, and record component temperatures with a thermocouple or an infrared camera.

Measurement tips

  • Use differential voltage probes for floating nodes. Connecting the ground clip of a standard probe to a floating node can short the circuit through the oscilloscope ground.
  • Use a current probe for inductor and switch currents.
  • Keep the probe ground leads short to avoid measuring ringing that is not really there.

Common problems and what they indicate

  • Strong ringing on the drain voltage: too much loop inductance in the layout.
  • A hot diode: reverse-recovery loss.
  • An inductor current that bends upward within a switching period: core saturation.
  • Erratic controller behavior: switching noise coupling into the signal ground.
